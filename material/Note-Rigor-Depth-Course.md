# A Note on the Rigor and Depth of this Course

* **Simplicity has limits ("Simple, but not simpler"):**  
  Our goal is to make every complex concept as accessible as possible. However, deep learning relies on fundamental mathematics, optimization theory, and intricate architectural design. Stripping these away would turn the subject into hollow buzzwords. As the principle often attributed to Albert Einstein reminds us:  
  > *“Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler.”*  
  ([Quote Investigator: Einstein on Simplicity](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2011/05/13/einstein-simple/))  
  Watering down the material further would compromise your actual ability to understand and work with these models.

* **There are no shortcuts to mastery:**  
  Struggling through debugging loss curves, and understanding low-level mechanics is an essential part of the learning process. Over two thousand years ago, when King Ptolemy asked if there was an easier way to master math, Euclid famously answered:  
  > *“There is no royal road to geometry.”*  
  ([Socratic Method: Euclid's "No Royal Road"](https://www.socratic-method.com/quote-meanings/euclid-there-is-no-royal-road-to-geometry))  
  Similarly, there is no “royal road” to deep learning. True competence requires putting in the intellectual effort.

* **Engineers vs. Executives (Why we don't teach this like an IoT overview):**  
  When teaching applied ML to students in other domains (such as IoT), an intuitive, high-level conceptual approach is appropriate—much like the famous UC Berkeley course [*Physics for Future Presidents*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_for_Future_Presidents), which teaches non-physicists how to understand technology at a policy level.  
  *However, AI students.*  are not being trained to merely oversee or consume AI, but to be the **engineers and scientists** who design, build, debug, and advance it. That requires moving past surface-level intuition and mastering the underlying rigor.
